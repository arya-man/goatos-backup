# GOAT LIFECYCLE

Every goat on this farm moves through the same stages of life. Understanding this helps you understand why animals are fed, managed, and housed differently depending on where they are in their lifecycle.

| 1 | Birth (Kidding)<br>Litter size: Most adult females give birth to 1–3 kids. Twins are most common.<br>Duration: Active labour typically lasts 30–60 minutes. Kids are born front hooves and nose first.<br>Alert: If a doe has been straining for more than 30 minutes with no kid born, call your supervisor immediately. |
| --- | --- |

| 2 | Colostrum — First 48 Hours<br>What is colostrum: The first milk produced by the mother after birth. It is thick and yellowish in colour — different from regular milk.<br>Why it is critical: A newborn kid's immune system is completely blank. Colostrum is the only way it receives antibodies in early life.<br>The window: The kid must receive colostrum in the first few hours and throughout the first 48 hours. After this the kid's gut closes and can no longer absorb these antibodies. This window cannot be recovered.<br>Alert: If a newborn kid is not feeding from its mother, flag it to a supervisor immediately — this is time-sensitive. |
| --- | --- |

| 3 | Milk Feeding<br>Duration: After colostrum, kids continue on milk until weaning — typically 60 to 90 days of age.<br>Healthy sign: Active, alert, full round belly after feeding. |
| --- | --- |

| 4 | Weaning<br>What it is: The process of transitioning the kid off milk onto solid feed — hay, concentrate, and water. Done gradually over several days.<br>Why gradually: The digestive system needs time to develop and adjust. Abrupt weaning causes stress and digestive problems.<br>Healthy sign: Kid eating solid feed confidently and maintaining weight. |
| --- | --- |

| 5 | Fattening<br>What it is: Once weaned, kids raised for meat enter the fattening phase. They receive a high-energy diet designed to maximise weight gain over a defined period.<br>Your role: Follow the feed schedule exactly as given. Never add or change feed without instruction. |
| --- | --- |

| 6 | Puberty & Breeding<br>Puberty: Female goats reach sexual maturity at around 10 months — when they are physically ready to be bred.<br>Signs of heat: Restlessness, loud bleating, rapid tail wagging, reduced appetite. | --- | --- |

| 7 | Productive Life & End of Fertility<br>Productive lifespan: A doe is generally fertile and productive up to around 8 years of age, after which reproductive performance declines.<br>Buck lifespan: Bucks remain fertile for a similar period but are typically assessed and rotated based on performance. |

# GOAT REPRODUCTION

| 1 | Estrus or Heat<br>What is Estrus or Heat: It is the period in a female goat’s reproductive cycle when she is sexually receptive and can be bred.<br>Duration: It is typically for 12-48 hours although 24 hours is the average.<br>Frequency: A goat comes into estrus once every 21 days on average whereas a sheep comes into estrus once every 17 days.<br>Synchronisation: It refers to syncing the estrus cycles of all the goats such that they come into estrus at the same time. With this delivery dates are more predictable as all that come into estrus can be mated at the same time. We can synchronise estrus cycles using progesterone sponges kept in their vagina for 14 days.<br>Seasonality: Some goat breeds show estrus only in the winter seasons and are influenced by the changes in day light. They are therefore short day breeders, as they come to estrus as the length of the day becomes shorter. This is an evolutionary mechanism designed to ensure birth happens during the summers, a season which generally has better weather and feed availability as compared to winters.<br>Signs: A goat that is in estrus is generally restless and is very active. It may also make a lot of sounds. Sometimes white discharge can be observed from its vagina. |
| --- | --- |

| 2 | Breeding<br>Modes: There are different modes of breeding: natural and artificial insemination. Natural involves a buck naturally mating and artificial insemination involves doing a quick surgery to deposit semen into the uterine horns of the goat.<br>Natural breeding ratio: It is advised to use 1 buck for every 5 females that need to be bred for optimal results. The buck should be given a rest of 2 days at least before being used for the next breeding session.<br>Artificial insemination ratio: Fresh semen of a buck when diluted properly can be used in up to 100 females till 3 days post semen collection. |
| --- | --- |

| 3 | Gestation<br>What is gestation: It is the period when a goat carries kids in its womb. <br>Pregnancy confirmation: Pregnancy in a goat can only be confirmed using an ultrasound. We can ultrasound the goats starting 45 days from the day of breeding, and later scans around 3-4 months become difficult.<br>Gestation period: It is about 150 days, i.e. it takes 150 days from the day of breeding for the goat to deliver its kids. |
| --- | --- |

| 4 | Anestrus<br>What is anestrus: It is the period after delivery or during off season in which a goat does not come into estrus in its expected once every 21 days fashion.<br>Duration: If it is not season related, then about 60 days post delivery a goat is expected to start its regular estrus cycles again. If it is seasonal, then estrus cycles resume once the on season begins. |

# ANIMAL IDENTIFICATION

Ear Tags: Every animal on the farm has a numbered ear tag. This is the animal's identity on the farm.

RFID: Each tag carries an RFID chip linked to the animal's full record — weight history, health, breeding data, and more.

Always use the tag number: Never refer to an animal as 'the black goat in pen 3.' Always use the tag number when reporting or discussing any animal.

Missing tag: If an animal's tag is missing, damaged, or unreadable, report immediately.

# THE DIGESTIVE SYSTEM

Goats have a four-chamber stomach — very different from humans. Understanding this explains most of the feeding rules on the farm.

| Chamber | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Rumen (largest) | The fermentation tank. Feed is broken down here by billions of microbes. The visible bulge on the left side of the goat is the rumen underneath. |
| 2. Reticulum | Works alongside the rumen. Also traps foreign objects the goat swallows accidentally. |
| 3. Omasum | Absorbs water from the feed before it moves forward. |
| 4. Abomasum | The true stomach — uses acid to digest proteins, similar to a human stomach. |

Cud Chewing (Rumination)

After eating, goats bring food back up from the rumen and chew it again slowly while resting. This is called chewing their cud. A goat that is chewing cud is a healthy, settled goat.

Normal: 6–8 hours of cud chewing per day

Red flag: A goat not chewing cud is almost always unwell - report it

Feed Changes Must Be Gradual

The rumen is full of microbes adapted to the current diet. A sudden change — even between batches of the same feed — can cause severe digestive upset, bloat, or death. All feed transitions at VGoat are managed carefully. Follow the schedule exactly as given.

Bloat

Bloat is gas buildup in the rumen that the goat cannot release. It can be fatal within hours.

Sign: Firm, swollen left side of the belly — sounds like a drum when tapped

Action: Alert your supervisor immediately

Aging by Teeth

Goats only have lower front teeth — the upper front jaw is a hard pad with no teeth. Age can be roughly estimated from the lower front teeth.

| Age | What the teeth look like |
| --- | --- |
| Under 1 year | 8 small narrow baby (milk) teeth |
| ~1 year | Centre 2 baby teeth replaced by 2 larger adult teeth |
| ~2 years | 4 adult teeth visible |
| ~3 years | 6 adult teeth visible |
| ~4 years + | All 8 are adult teeth — called a 'full mouth' |

# GOAT BEHAVIOUR

Social Nature

Goats are herd animals — they get stressed when isolated. Never separate a single goat from the group for long without good reason.

Every herd has a dominance hierarchy. Dominant animals eat first and claim the best spots. This is normal.

When new animals are introduced, head-butting and chasing will happen until the hierarchy re-establishes itself — also normal.

Stress

Stress directly impacts health, digestion, and weight gain. Common stressors in a stall setting:

Loud noises, shouting, or sudden movements near the pens

Irregular feeding times — goats have strong internal clocks

Rough or careless handling

Note: Always move slowly and calmly around the animals. Let them see you before you touch them. Never shout or run inside the park.

What Their Sounds Mean

| Sound | Likely meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Soft bleat | Calling to her kid, or greeting a familiar person |
| Loud repeated bleat | Distress, hunger, pain, or isolation — investigate |
| Snorting / blowing | Alarm — something has startled the animal |
| Teeth grinding | Pain or discomfort — flag to the health team |
| Quiet gurgling | Normal rumen activity — all is fine |

# MEDICINE ADMINISTRATION

You will regularly see medicines being given to animals. This section explains each method so you are not confused when you see it. Administering medicine is not your responsibility unless you are specifically trained and instructed to do so.

Injections

| Type | What it means | Where it is given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IM — Intramuscular | Injected directly into the muscle | Neck or hindquarter muscle |
| SQ — Subcutaneous | Injected just under the skin, not into the muscle | Loose skin on the neck or behind the shoulder |
| IV — Intravenous | Injected directly into a vein — used for fluids or fast-acting drugs | Jugular vein in the neck |
| Oral | To be given orally | Mouth |

Stomach Tubing

A thin flexible tube is passed through the nose or mouth and down into the rumen or stomach. Used when a kid is too weak to suckle and needs colostrum delivered directly, or when a goat needs urgent fluids or medication. It looks alarming if you haven't seen it before — it is a routine and safe procedure when done correctly by a trained person.

Oral Drenching

Liquid medicine or supplements are given directly into the mouth using a syringe or drench gun. The goat's head is tilted slightly and the liquid is delivered to the back of the mouth. Used for dewormers, vitamins, and electrolytes.

# WEIGHING

Weight is one of the most important data points on the farm. It tells us if a fattening kid is on track, if an animal is losing condition, or if a breeding doe is healthy. Accurate weighing and logging is non-negotiable.

| Animal | Weighing Schedule |
| --- | --- |
| Kids | Every Monday |
| Adult Goats | On the 15th of every month |

Your role: When weighing is happening in your area, help move and hold animals calmly and ensure the tag number is correctly recorded against the weight logged.

# GOATS WILL EAT ANYTHING

Goats are curious and will mouth and swallow almost anything they can reach. In a stall setting this is a genuine danger.

No plastic bags, wrappers, rope ends, wire, or loose cloth near or inside the pens

Do not bring in grass, leaves, or any feed from outside — even as a well-meaning treat

Check your pockets and loose clothing before entering pens

If you see a goat chewing something it shouldn't be, remove it and report if any was swallowed

# BASIC HANDLING & RESTRAINT

Approach: Always from the side — never directly from the front or from behind

Holding: Place one arm under the neck and use your body to guide the animal against a wall or corner

Catching: Back the animal gently into a corner — chasing causes stress and injury to both animal and handler

Horns: Never grab an animal by the horns

Bucks: Always be aware of where a buck is facing. Never turn your back on a dominant buck.

Kids: Support the full body when lifting — never pick up a kid by one leg
